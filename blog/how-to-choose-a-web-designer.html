How to Choose a Web Designer for Your Business

July 2026 · Sohaib Younas

Hiring a web designer feels risky when you cannot code and cannot judge quality from a screenshot alone. This guide breaks the decision into checks you can actually run before you pay anyone.

Start with the portfolio, not the pitch

Anyone can talk about "modern, responsive design." Ask instead for three live sites the designer built, ideally in your industry or close to it. Open each one on your phone. Check load speed, check if text is readable without zooming, and check if buttons are easy to tap. A portfolio full of nice screenshots but slow, broken live sites tells you the truth.

Ask what happens after launch

A website is not a one-time delivery. Domains renew, plugins need updates, and content changes. Before hiring, ask directly: who owns the code, who hosts it, and what happens if you want a small text change six months later. A designer who has a clear answer, even if it costs extra, is more trustworthy than one who avoids the question.

Check for SEO awareness, not just visuals

A beautiful site that never shows up on Google is not doing its job. Ask if the designer sets up basic on-page SEO by default, things like page titles, meta descriptions, image alt text, and a sitemap. If the answer is "SEO is a separate service," that is fine, just budget for it separately instead of assuming it is included.

Compare pricing on scope, not just the number

A 150 dollar quote and a 1500 dollar quote are not comparable unless you know what each includes. Get a written scope: number of pages, number of revisions, whether the price includes hosting setup, and whether stock images or copywriting are included. Cheap quotes that skip these details often cost more later in change requests.

Red flags worth walking away from

  • No live examples of past work, only PDF mockups.
  • Pressure to pay the full amount upfront before any contract or scope document.
  • Vague answers about who will own the final source files.
  • No mention of mobile responsiveness unless you ask first.

The short version

Check real live sites, get a written scope, ask about ownership and maintenance, and confirm SEO basics are covered. A designer who answers these clearly, without getting defensive, is usually the safer choice.
